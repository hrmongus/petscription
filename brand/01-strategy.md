# Howl & Oats — Brand Strategy

## The one-liner
**Howl & Oats** — cold-pressed, batch-dated dog kibble made from a short list of
named ingredients. Milled in small runs. Dated like bread.

## The gap in the shelf
Premium dog food splits into two tired camps:

| Camp | Signals | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vet-clinical** | White bags, sans-serif, "scientifically formulated" | Reads like medication. No pleasure, no provenance. |
| **Wolf-cosplay** | Black bags, mountains, "ancestral", grain-free | Sells a fantasy the customer knows is a fantasy. |

Nobody is selling dog food the way good coffee, bread, and olive oil are sold:
**by naming the farm, dating the batch, and shutting up about the rest.**

## Positioning
> For the person who reads the label on their own groceries and then feels bad
> reading their dog's, Howl & Oats is small-batch kibble with a paper trail —
> cold-pressed, batch-dated, and grain-*positive* — because the shortest
> ingredient list you can pronounce beats the longest one you can't.

## The contrarian bet: grain-positive
The category ran to "grain-free" and stayed there. We run the other way, loudly
and specifically: **named heritage grains, named farms.** Rolled oats, hulled
barley, whole rye. Not "ancient grains" — *these* grains, from *that* mill,
rolled *this* month.

It gives us the name, the color palette, the smell of the store, and a fight to
pick. That is worth more than another wolf on another black bag.

## Audience
**Primary — "The Label Reader" (28–45, urban/inner-suburb).**
Buys single-origin coffee, keeps a sourdough starter, has opinions about olive
oil. Owns one dog and treats it as a roommate with a strong personality, not a
child. Will pay 30–40% more for legible provenance. Buys the aesthetic *and*
audits the claims — so every claim must survive being audited.

**Secondary — "The Convert."** Came for a specific problem (itchy skin, senior
joints, a fussy eater), stayed because the bag looks good on the counter and
doesn't have to live in a cupboard.

## Brand idea
### *Someone in this relationship should be paying attention.*
The dog does not care. The dog would eat a shoe. We do the work anyway. That
tension — enormous care aimed at an audience that is entirely indifferent — is
the whole brand, and it's why the tone is dry instead of sentimental.

## Pillars
1. **Milled, not manufactured.** Runs of 400 kg. Cold-pressed at 45–60 °C so the
   fats and the enzymes survive the process.
2. **A paper trail.** Every bag stamped: batch number, mill date, best-by.
   Batch number resolves to a page listing every farm in that run.
3. **Grain-positive.** Named heritage grains, credited like ingredients on a
   menu, not hidden like a compromise.
4. **Dry, not cute.** No fur babies. No exclamation points. The dog keeps its
   dignity; so does the buyer.

## Proof, not adjectives
Every pillar has to cash out in something physical, or it gets cut:

- *Milled, not manufactured* → run size printed on the bag; mill day is Tuesday
  and we say so.
- *A paper trail* → `howlandoats.com/batch/0412` lists the farms in that run.
- *Grain-positive* → grain supplier named on the front of the bag, not the back.
- *Dry, not cute* → the back-of-bag manifesto. It's the ad.

## What we will not do
- Not sell fear (no "toxins", no scare-quoted "kibble").
- Not anthropomorphize. The dog is a dog. That's the good part.
- Not use the word *artisanal*. We are. We don't say it.
- Not do a black bag with a wolf on it.
